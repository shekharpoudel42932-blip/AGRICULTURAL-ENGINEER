I grew up on a few acres of paddy and wheat, where the year ran on the monsoon calendar and every conversation at
dinner eventually turned to water: when it would come, how much, and whose turn it was at the canal. I didn't know
the word "engineering" then, but I spent most afternoons taking apart my father's diesel pump to work out why it
coughed every time the water table dropped.

## The dry year

In 2014 the rains failed. I watched our neighbours pump day and night, burning diesel they couldn't afford, while
half the water ran off the far end of the field before it reached the roots. Our yield fell by almost half. It was
the first time I understood that the problem wasn't only *how much* water we had, but how badly we were using it.
That summer I decided I wanted to be the person who could fix that.

## Learning the language

Agricultural engineering gave me words and tools for what I had seen as a kid: hydraulics for the pump that
coughed, soil physics for the water that vanished, and machine design for the tools that wore out my father's back.
My capstone was a pedal-operated thresher built from scrap steel by a village welder. It was crude, but I learned
something I still believe: a solution only counts if farmers can afford it, repair it and trust it.

> The best irrigation system is the one a farmer still uses five years after the engineer has left.

## Into the field

My first job put me in orchards and vegetable clusters, designing drip systems and teaching fertigation under the
shade of a mango tree. I learned more from farmers' questions than from any textbook. Why does the last row always
dry out? Why does the pump trip at noon? Every question became a small design problem, and every fix earned a
little more trust.

## What drives me now

Today my research ties low-cost soil sensors to satellite data so irrigation can respond to what the crop actually
needs, not to the calendar. I still think about that dry summer and the diesel smoke drifting over the fields.
What keeps me going is simple: helping one more farmer grow a full harvest with less water, less energy and less
worry, and then sharing what worked so the next engineer can start further down the road.
